The Reviewer’s Problem

Used, but not usefully

R
Rick Musial Founder & Chairman, DVS
23 November 2026
4 min read

The closure report says the system went live. People are logging in. The steering committee has thanked the team and moved on.

The question nobody has asked yet is whether any of it is creating value.

Most of my projects were in corporates and government. One I remember was the old document management system. It was implemented, and people used it. But nobody really wanted to spend the time to use it properly.

They were used to saving their work on their D drive.

Being used and creating value are different things.

What value meant

The business case for a system like that promises outcomes: the right version found quickly, a record you can rely on, less time spent looking. Those outcomes only arrive if people do the part that takes a little longer, every time.

The extra time is where it broke.

People used the parts that were useful to themselves: somewhere to save their own documents and find them again. The parts that were useful to the organisation as a whole, the filing and the details that let someone else find the record next year, took longer and helped someone else.

So they were the parts that got skipped, and plenty of documents never left the D drive at all.

In a sense, people were behaving quite rationally. The extra work was theirs, but much of the benefit belonged to someone else, perhaps someone they would never meet.

What they didn’t understand, because nobody had shown them, was that the organisation’s value was sitting on their D drive. The knowledge, the records, the version someone else would need: the system was there to move it somewhere the organisation could use it.

Kept on a D drive, it was only ever useful to the person who saved it.

A login count won’t show you that. The system is busy. The value isn’t there.

People used the parts that were useful to themselves.

Trying it again

The system had been rolled out once and hadn’t been used well. Later I was in a meeting with the assistant secretary for IT, who was planning to implement it again.

I suggested that implementing it the same way a second time would most likely produce the same result.

The response was that they would do it exactly the same way as before.

I remember thinking of that old saying about doing the same thing repeatedly and expecting a different result.

There wasn’t much more to say.

The organisation had already seen what happened the first time. Yet the plan was to repeat the implementation without changing the approach that had produced the result.

It wasn’t just us

In June 2012, the Australian National Audit Office published Records Management in the Australian Public Service.

Drawing on a 2010 National Archives survey of 138 Australian Government agencies and bodies, it reported that in 2009 fewer than 30 per cent of them managed most of their records digitally, even though more than half reported having an electronic document and records management system.

In the three agencies it examined, it found that information for a business activity was often held in a variety of locations and systems, and that staff generally had limited understanding of information outside their day-to-day responsibilities.

The systems existed. The organisational practices needed to realise their value often didn’t.

Newer tools haven’t changed the habit. The D drive just has a new name. It’s called OneDrive.

The organisation’s value was sitting on their D drive.

What it looks like from your desk

If you review business cases or sit on a steering committee, the signs are quiet.

The go-live report counts users, not outcomes. The benefit in the case is described as the system itself. And nobody can say, in a sentence, what using it properly would look like.

The underlying problem is often an incentive nobody considered.

The person who has to do the additional work isn’t necessarily the person who receives the benefit. Unless that is addressed, the business case may be depending on behaviour that nobody has a good reason to adopt.

And once the project is closed, who is still watching?

Ask these before the money is approved

  • What does using it properly mean, in one sentence?
  • What happens to the old way? If the D: drive or the network drives are still there on day one, which will people use?
  • Who does the extra work, and who gets the benefit? If they’re different people, what makes the first one do it?
  • What will be counted to show proper use, not just use?
  • Who looks again at six months, and who do they tell?
  • If this has been tried before, what is different in the plan this time?

A system can be used by everyone and still fail to deliver the value that justified buying it.

The value is in how it’s used, and someone has to decide what that means before the money is approved.

Source: Australian National Audit Office, Records Management in the Australian Public Service, Audit Report No.53 2011–12, 27 June 2012.
