Behind the Build

I wrote a plan I could never have run

R
Rick Musial Founder & Chairman, DVS
7 September 2026
4 min read

If you’re a founder sitting on a plan you wrote months ago and still haven’t started, mine is six weeks old and has produced nothing at all.

On 12 July I wrote a production spec for a video series. A hundred and twenty-two lines: a premise, six brand rules, a six-beat format with timings, four episodes scripted end to end, roles assigned, a distribution plan, a first-week action list.

As a document it holds up. I reread it twice this week and I’d sign off on every line again.

Not one frame has been filmed.

The obvious explanation is discipline, and the obvious explanation is wrong. Over the same period I’ve published thirty-one posts, built the tooling that audits my own record, and closed two hundred and twenty-seven working sessions.

The video series isn’t sitting still because I can’t make myself work.

The thing I never checked

I spent an hour this week pulling apart a podcast that ships an episode a week without visible strain. The useful part wasn’t the subject matter. It was the arithmetic of who supplies what.

The host doesn’t write the episode – she answers a co-host, so the script arrives as questions. She doesn’t originate all the material either: one episode is a filmed version of a class she already teaches every year. The audience supplies future material by sending things in to be looked at. The credential that makes anyone click sits in the title.

What’s left for the expert is the single input nobody else can supply: knowing the answer.

Every hard input in that format is supplied by somebody other than the person who is hardest to schedule.

Read my own spec against that and it’s the mirror image.

I write the episodes. I perform them alone, scripted, to camera. I supply the subject matter. I write the companion newsletter.

The document says the production runs on one and a half people, then hands the one person every input that has to exist before anything can ship.

Complete and runnable are different tests

I graded that spec on completeness.

Does it have a format? Yes. Timings? Yes. Are the episodes written? All four. Roles assigned? Yes.

By every test I applied, it was finished.

Nobody, me included, asked the other question: given who is actually available, can this be executed at all?

It couldn’t.

The format I’d specified was a short, scripted solo piece to camera – the hardest possible first format for someone who had never edited video. My own notes from a week later say exactly that: no camera hardware, no editing software, no video experience.

Both facts were written down in the same folder. I never set them side by side.

A finished-looking plan is harder to see through than a rough one. Completeness reads as readiness.

The question to ask before you write the plan

Take the thing you keep not starting.

For each input it needs – the words, the performance, the raw material, the distribution, the money – write down who supplies it.

If your name is on every line, more discipline may not change anything. You’re the constraint, and you’ve routed every dependency through yourself.

Then ask the cheaper follow-up: what have I already made that this could be built from?

The class you already teach. The document you already wrote. The four scripts sitting finished in a folder.

A new format should be a camera pointed at existing work rather than a commission for more of it.

The material existed the whole time. What stopped me was a format that asked me to perform it alone, in the one medium I’d never worked in.

Two people reading and arguing about a verdict that’s already on paper needs no new script and almost no editing.

Same material. Different supplier list.

Now it can happen on a Tuesday.

I’ll report back in a month on whether it did.

R

Rick Musial
