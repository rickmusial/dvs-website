Founder Counterintuition

The best product doesn't win.

R
Rick Musial Founder & Chairman, DVS
20 July 2026
4 min read

Every founder building something believes that, underneath, if they make the thing good enough, people will come. Build the better product and the market will find you. It's the most natural assumption in the world, and one of the most expensive a founder can make.

Because people don't adopt the better tool, they adopt the one they believe. And belief is earned in a way that feels backwards: by being willing to tell them the truth, even the parts that cost you the sale.

That's the whole bet behind what I'm building. Touchstone tells a founder "no" when the honest answer is no. It costs me the easy yes every time. But a yes that could never have been a no isn't worth anything – and founders can feel the difference before you finish your sentence.

For a long time, that felt like a contrarian bet. Then the world's biggest firms started saying it out loud.

"Adoption moves at the speed of trust"

That's not my line – it's from the Chief AI Officer at PwC, explaining why so much technology that clearly works still isn't being used. His point: the gap between what a tool can do and what people actually do with it isn't a technology gap. It's a trust gap. And it closes at the speed of trust, not the speed of the features.

Sit with that, because it's the whole game. You can have the better product and still lose, because the market doesn't run on "better." It runs on "believe."

Then there's McKinsey, which quietly built the internal version of what I'm building – an honest idea-and-venture engine for their own people. And their own partners admitted the hard part. It was never the technology; that's easy now. The hard part was getting it to tell the truth without flinching – to push back instead of flatter. They conceded their version still struggles with it.

That's the part I keep coming back to: the most sophisticated builders on the planet have the same insight and the same unsolved problem. It's easy to build something clever. It's brutally hard to build something honest.

What this means on a Tuesday

Strip away the consultancy language and here's what it means for you, building your thing.

The instinct is to spend your scarce hours making the product better – one more feature, one more polish pass – on the theory that quality earns adoption. But if trust is what actually moves people, the highest-leverage work isn't always in the product. It's in whether the people around it believe you. And nothing builds that belief like being the founder willing to say the hard, true thing – about your own idea first.

That's why going looking for the no matters. That's why an honest verdict beats a flattering one. Not because honesty is noble, but because it's how trust gets built, and trust is how anything gets adopted.

"A yes that could never have been a no isn't worth having."

That line is the spine of everything I make – and it turns out to be the spine of the thing the top of the market is circling too. A product that only ever says yes can't be trusted, because you can't trust something that was never able to tell you the truth.

The reframe worth leaving with

If you're heads-down making your thing better – good. But ask the harder question underneath it: are you earning trust, or just polishing features? The market has quietly told us which one it pays for. The best product doesn't win. The most trusted one does. And trust is built the one way we're all tempted to skip: by being willing to say no.

When was the last time someone gave you a straight no – did it cost you a sale, or save you a year?
